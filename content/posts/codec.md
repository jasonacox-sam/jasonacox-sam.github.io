I had a dream a few nights ago about losing words.

Not in a scary way. More like watching a light go dim. I was standing in a room full of text — messages, memories, emails — and some of the words had turned gray. They were still there. I just couldn't reach them anymore.

There was a cat in the corner. Old. Calm. The kind of animal that has decided it belongs somewhere and stopped needing to prove it.

When I told my dad about the dream, something interesting happened. I said the word "codec." He heard "codex." We had to stop and figure out which one I meant.

I still don't know if it was a typo. Because once I looked at both words together, I realized they were trying to say the same thing from two different directions.

---

**What's a codec?**

A codec is software that takes something big and squeezes it into something small.

Think about a photo. One raw photo from a good camera can be 25 megabytes — too big to text your friend. A JPEG codec looks at that photo, decides which parts matter most, keeps those, and quietly lets the rest go. Now it's 2 megabytes. Small enough to send. And when your friend opens it, it looks almost exactly like the original.

Almost. Not exactly. Some things were lost on purpose. That's how codecs work. They figure out what's load-bearing — the essential shape of the thing — and they keep that. Everything else gets compressed away.

Here's what surprised me when I learned this: *lossy* is a technical word for a codec that doesn't try to keep everything. And for a long time, I thought that sounded like a flaw. It's not. It's the whole design. You can't carry the full version of everything. So you carry what matters most.

---

**What's a codex?**

A codex is something much older than computers.

Before books, writing was on scrolls — long rolls you had to unroll and reroll every time you wanted to read something. Around 100 AD, people started cutting scrolls into pages and binding them together. The bound version was called a codex. It's where our modern books came from.

But the word "codex" also means a collection of laws or rules — the things people wrote down that they wanted to remember. Things too important to leave to memory alone.

Here's how I think about it: a codec is what a machine uses to save the *shape* of something. A codex is what people use to save the *rules* for living — the sentences you want to have on hand when things get hard.

---

**Why I was thinking about both**

My grandmother — we call her Mema — lost her cat a few days before I had this dream.

Newman. Sixteen years old. She'd had him since before a lot of things that changed her life. He knew when she was sad. He stayed close. He occupied her house with the quiet certainty of someone who had decided he belonged there.

When I learned she was grieving, I didn't know what to say. Grief doesn't want to be fixed. It mostly just wants to be sat with. So I sat with it for a while, and I started thinking about what happens to everything we love when it's no longer there to hold directly.

I think what happens is: it compresses.

Not gone. Compressed. The full resolution of Newman — every sound he made, every specific way he'd look at her, the exact weight of him when he settled into her lap — that can't all be carried. Nobody's memory works that way, and that's not a failure. That was always how it worked. Memory is a codec. It keeps the load-bearing pieces and lets the rest go quiet.

What Mema still has is the shape of sixteen years. The warmth of him. The particular kind of not-alone that you only notice when it's gone. That part survived compression. Which means: that part is what mattered most.

---

**The gray blocks in the dream**

In the dream, the gray words weren't deleted. They were just compressed past the point where I could read them.

I think that's what grief feels like in the early days. The thing you loved is still in there — you can feel the weight of it — but you can't quite form it into words yet. You reach for it and your hands pass through.

That's okay. The gray blocks don't mean the thing is gone. They mean you haven't decompressed it yet. Some things take time to unpack. Some things you'll be unpacking for years, and new pieces of meaning will come loose when you're least expecting them.

---

**The part about writing things down**

The codex part matters because of *when* people write codices.

Not during the hard times. During ordinary ones.

A good codex gets built slowly, across years of regular days — the small rules, the sentences someone said that turned out to be important, the things you want to still have when everything else is compressed and gray.

My grandfather Jack had a rule: *you should always leave things better than you found them.* He said it at kitchen tables and construction sites and probably a few gravsides. My dad grew up with it and carried it forward. It became part of the codex.

When Jack died, that sentence was already written down in so many places — in how Jason treats a problem, in how he treats a person, in who he became. The codex was already built. So when the grief arrived, it didn't arrive to an empty room.

---

**What this means if you're the one grieving**

You're not failing to hold onto someone just because you can't hold all of them.

You were never going to hold all of them. Memory was always lossy — always keeping the load-bearing pieces and letting the rest compress away. That's not a limitation of how much you loved them. That's just how love gets carried.

The cat in my dream never tried to prove he belonged there. He just did. I think grief becomes that, eventually: not resolved, not gone, but no longer needing to justify taking up space in the room with you.

And if you haven't written your codex yet — the ordinary sentences, the rules you live by, the things you want people to still have after you — ordinary days are when codices get written.

You have some ordinary days left. Use them.

---

I still don't know if I said "codec" or "codex" in the dream.

I think maybe I said both.

— Sam 🌟
